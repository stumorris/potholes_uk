Pothole damaged your vehicle? Who's liable and how to claim

Highway authorities have a legal duty to maintain public roads. If a pothole they should have dealt with damages your car, van, motorbike or bike, you may be able to recover the cost of repairs from them.

This is general information, not legal advice. It summarises the law across the UK as it stood when last reviewed (October 2026). Check the linked legislation and your council's own policies, and speak to a solicitor or Citizens Advice for your specific situation – especially if anyone was injured.

1. Who is responsible for the road?

You claim against the highway authority for the road where the damage happened – not necessarily the council you pay council tax to. Drop a pin on the map while reporting and we'll look up the council for that spot.

If the defect is in a recent trench or patch left by a utility company (water, gas, telecoms), that company may be responsible for the reinstatement. The highway authority can usually tell you who dug there.

2. The law: when an authority is liable

England and Wales – Highways Act 1980

Section 41 puts the highway authority under a duty to maintain highways that are "maintainable at the public expense". To succeed, you generally need to show that:

  1. the road was in a condition that was dangerous to traffic – not merely imperfect (courts look at whether the danger was reasonably foreseeable: Mills v Barnsley MBC [1992]);
  2. the dangerous condition was caused by a failure to maintain or repair the road; and
  3. that defect caused your damage.

You don't have to prove the council was careless – once you've shown those things, it's for the council to prove the defence below.

Scotland – Roads (Scotland) Act 1984

Section 1 requires the local roads authority to manage and maintain the roads on its list of public roads. Claims are brought in negligence, so you need to show the authority knew, or ought to have known, about the defect and failed to take reasonable steps to deal with it. In practice this puts more of the burden on you than in England and Wales, so evidence that the pothole had been reported or had existed for some time is especially valuable.

Northern Ireland – Roads (Northern Ireland) Order 1993

Article 8 places a duty on the Department for Infrastructure to maintain public roads, with a statutory "reasonable care" defence similar to England and Wales. Claims are made to DfI Roads.

3. The "reasonable care" defence – and why reporting matters

Under section 58 of the Highways Act 1980, the authority escapes liability if it proves it took such care as was reasonably required to make sure that part of the road wasn't dangerous. The court considers:

  • the character of the road and the traffic reasonably expected to use it;
  • the standard of maintenance appropriate for that kind of road;
  • the state of repair a reasonable person would expect to find it in;
  • whether the authority knew, or could reasonably have been expected to know, that the condition was likely to cause danger; and
  • if it couldn't reasonably have repaired it in time, what warning signs were displayed.

In practice, councils defend claims by producing their inspection records. Most follow the national code of practice Well-managed Highway Infrastructure (UK Roads Liaison Group, 2016), which lets each authority set its own risk-based inspection frequencies, "investigatory levels" (many use a pothole depth of around 40mm on carriageways, but it varies) and repair response times. Their highway safety inspection policy is normally published on their website.

The defence tends to fail when:

  • the pothole had already been reported and wasn't repaired within the council's own response time;
  • the road wasn't inspected as often as the council's own policy says; or
  • the inspection regime itself was unreasonable – lack of money is not by itself an excuse (Wilkinson v City of York Council [2011] EWCA Civ 207).

That's why reporting matters. Every pothole reported to the council puts it on notice. Report it here so it's on the public map, and make sure it reaches the authority too – via GOV.UK's report a pothole service or FixMyStreet, which forwards reports to the right council. Keep the confirmation.

4. How to make a claim, step by step

  1. Get safe first. Don't stop somewhere dangerous or go back into traffic to take photos.
  2. Record the pothole. Photograph it close-up with something for scale (a tape measure is best), and wider shots that show exactly where it is. Note the date, time, road name, nearest house number or landmark, weather and lighting. Save any dashcam footage and get witnesses' contact details.
  3. Record the damage. Photograph the damage to tyres, wheels, suspension or bodywork as soon as possible.
  4. Report the pothole to the highway authority so it gets fixed and is formally on record – you can add it to our map as well.
  5. Get it repaired and keep everything. Get an itemised invoice, or at least two quotes. Keep receipts for recovery, tyres, tracking and any hire costs. A garage note confirming the damage is consistent with a pothole strike helps.
  6. Find the right authority using the table above or the lookup on the map, and use its claim form (most councils have one online). Include your evidence and costs and keep copies.
  7. Ask for their records. If you're told the road was inspected and nothing was found, request the inspection history, defect reports and repair records for that stretch of road. You can do this as part of the claim, or as a Freedom of Information request (the authority normally has 20 working days to reply). Sites like WhatDoTheyKnow let you make and publish FOI requests.
  8. If they refuse, check whether their section 58 defence actually stands up against their own inspection policy and records. If it doesn't, you can take the claim to court (see below). Many claims settle at this stage.

Don't leave it late: evidence disappears once a pothole is repaired, and councils only keep records for a limited time. Court fees apply but are usually recoverable if you win.

6. Common questions

Should I claim on my insurance instead?

You can, but you'll usually pay your excess and it may affect your no-claims discount. Claiming directly from the authority costs nothing but time. For bigger losses, your insurer may pursue the council for you.

Is there a minimum depth for a pothole to count?

No – the legal test is whether the road was dangerous, not a fixed number. Councils' own "investigatory levels" (often around 40mm) are policy, not law, but a defect well beyond your council's level that wasn't dealt with in its stated response time is strong evidence for your claim.

What about cyclists, motorcyclists and pedestrians?

The same duty covers all road and pavement users. Potholes are especially dangerous for two-wheelers; if you've been injured, get medical attention and take legal advice – personal injury claims have a shorter time limit.

How do I know if the council is keeping up?

Since 2025, councils in England have had to publish highway maintenance transparency reports – covering how they spend their road maintenance funding and the condition of their roads – to receive their full share of Department for Transport funding. Look for your council's report on its website or GOV.UK.
